Predict: Why does graphene have three nearest neighbours while diamond has four?
Reveal explanationGraphene is approximately sp²-bonded in a plane, leaving a p orbital involved in the delocalised π system. Diamond is sp³-bonded, with four tetrahedrally arranged neighbours.

Predict: What happens to the slopes of the bands near K if the hopping energy increases?
Reveal explanationIncreasing t steepens the dispersion near K. The valence and conduction bands still touch at the Dirac point in this ideal nearest-neighbour model; increasing t does not open a gap.
Why are these called Dirac cones?Close to K, E ≈ ±ℏvF|q|, where q is the displacement from K. The dispersion is linear in |q|, unlike the approximately quadratic dispersion of conventional non-relativistic electrons.

Ideal zone-folding rule: if (n − m) is divisible by 3, the tube is nominally metallic; otherwise it is semiconducting. Curvature may open a small gap in non-armchair nominally metallic tubes.
Predict: Can two nanotubes made entirely of carbon have different electronic properties? Compare (5,5) and (7,5).
Reveal explanationYes. Rolling graphene imposes periodic boundary conditions around the circumference, restricting allowed wavevectors. Depending on chirality, the allowed lines in reciprocal space may pass through a graphene Dirac point (nominally metallic) or miss it (semiconducting).

Important: A carbon nanotube is a hollow, often ballistic, quasi-one-dimensional conductor. The bulk-wire expression R = L/(σπr²) is not a reliable physical model for an individual nanotube. Use this activity to explore a hypothetical cylindrical conductor; nanotube transport needs a quantum transport model.
Predict: Why can a thinner conductor dissipate less total power at the same current density, yet much more power at the same current?
Reveal explanationAt fixed current, P = I²L/(σA), so decreasing area increases power loss. At fixed current density J, I = JA and P = J²AL/σ, so a smaller cross-sectional area carries less current and dissipates less total power. These comparisons assume the same bulk conductivity and a uniform cylindrical conductor.

Model assumptions

Lattice: ideal flat honeycomb geometry. Bands: nearest-neighbour tight binding, no interactions or substrate. Nanotubes: ideal zone folding with curvature caveat. Transport: classical Drude and bulk-cylinder models, not full ballistic or quantum conductance.
